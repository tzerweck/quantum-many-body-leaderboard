                     Neuralized Fermionic Tensor Networks for Quantum Many-Body Systems

                                               Si-Jing  Du,1,∗  Ao  Chen,2  and  Garnet  Kin-Lic  Chan2,†
              1Division of Engineering and Applied Science, California Institute of Technology, Pasadena, California 91125, USA
           2Division of Chemistry and Chemical Engineering, California Institute of Technology, Pasadena, California 91125, USA

                     We describe a class of neuralized fermionic tensor network states (NN-fTNS) that introduce non-linearity
                  into fermionic tensor networks through configuration-dependent neural network transformations of the local
                  tensors.    The  construction  uses  the  fTNS  algebra  to  implement  a  natural  fermionic  sign  structure  and  is
                  compatible with standard tensor network algorithms, but gains enhanced expressivity through the neural network
                  parametrization.  Using the 1D and 2D Fermi-Hubbard models as benchmarks, we demonstrate that NN-fTNS
                  achieve order of magnitude improvements in the ground-state energy compared to pure fTNS with the same
                  bond dimension, and can be systematically improved through both the tensor network bond dimension and the
                  neural network parametrization.  Compared to existing fermionic neural quantum states (NQS) based on Slater
                  determinants and Pfaffians, NN-fTNS offer a physically motivated alternative fermionic structure. Furthermore,
                  compared to such states,  NN-fTNS naturally exhibit improved computational scaling and we demonstrate a
                  construction that achieves linear scaling with the lattice size.


   IntroductionFinding  compact  representations  of  quan-
tum  many-body  wavefunctions  remains  a  central  challenge
in  the  study  of  strongly  correlated  quantum  systems  [1–6].
In previous work [6], we introduced tensor network function
(TNF) representations of quantum states.  These expand the
well-known tensor network states (TNS) [2,3,7–9] to more
general functions of the input, capturing new kinds of physics
such  as  volume  law  entanglement,  while  still  allowing  for
a  connection  to  and  the  reuse  of  existing  tensor  network
algorithms.

fermionic quantum many-body problems which have a sign

aZ   2  graded  tensor  product  to  encode  the  sign  structure
and  have  been  demonstrated  to  be  a  powerful  Ansatz  for

   Scaling to larger systemsThe cost to compute an ampli-
tude in a NN-fTNS isO(N) whereNis the system size [19,
44,58].   This can be contrasted with theO(N                          3) scaling for
a  Slater  determinant  or  Pfaffian  NQS  [5].    However,  in  a
Markov Chain VMC calculation, typically a series of related
configurations are generated in a sweep [59], for example in
the computation of the local energy.   In a pure fTNS VMC
calculation, computation can be reused (in the form of partial
tensor network contraction intermediates [42]) which means
that for a local Hamiltonian (such as the FH model), the cost
per  VMC  energy  and  step  is  stillO(N)  [6, 60].   However,
the  global  configuration  dependence  of  a  NN-fTNS  means
that  even  local  configuration  changes  in  the  amplitude  lead
to  a  global  modification  of  the  tensors,  preventing  re-use
and leading to anO(N            2) scaling for each VMC step.   (This
is  related  to  theO(N             4)  scaling  of  a  VMC  step  with  Slater
determinant/Pfaffian NQS when reuse is not possible [30,32,
33, 61]).    This  is  illustrated  in  the  time  cost  comparisons
between NN-fPEPS and fPEPS for various bond dimensions

for the amplitude⟨n|Ψ⟩in Fig.S1. We emphasize that the neural network architecture employed in this study may not represent

Apart from the preliminary observation that increasing the number of hidden neurons in the MLP blocks can improve the model
expressivity, it remains an open and valuable direction for future work to systematically investigate the impact of neural network
architecture on the performance of NN-fTNS.

                                                                        Other NN based models

   We summarize below the architectural details of the various NN-based models used in Fig.2of the main text.
   For the pure NN model, we replace the fTNS structure in Fig.S1(a) with a two-layer MLP employing a shifted hyperbolic
sine activation function,f(x)=sinh(x)+1 [64], which directly outputs a real scalar value NN(n) representing the amplitude for
configuration|n⟩. In VMC optimization, the pure NN model parameters are initialized randomly.
   For  the  NN-SD  model,  we  follow  the  standard  neural  network  backflow  construction  [5, 53, 61],  replacing  the  fTNS  in
Fig.S1with a single-particle orbital matrixM                              i,k ∈R   No×N    e, whereN              o denotes the total number of spin orbitals andN        e is the total
number of electrons. Given a configuration|n⟩, the NN modifies the orbital matrix to produce a configuration-dependent matrix
M(n).  The rows ofM(n) corresponding to the occupied orbitals in|n⟩are selected to form a square matrix, whose determinant
det(M  io,j ) yields the final amplitude, wherei              o labels the occupied orbitals.  In VMC optimization, the NN-SD is initialized by
first fully optimizing the Slater determinant orbital matrix (via minimizing its mean-field energy in the FH model), after which
the NN parameters are initialized such that the neural network introduces only small perturbations to the fully optimized Slater
determinant.  The specific NN structures used are (1) NN-SD (MLP): a two-layer multilayer perceptron; (2) NN-SD (ViT): a
Vision Transformer [52] as shown in Fig.S1(a); (3) a convolutional neural network [57,65].
   For the fPEPS×NN-Jastrow model, given a configuration|n⟩and an fPEPS state|Ψ⟩, we apply the pure NN model to compute
a scalar factor NN(n), which is multiplied with the fPEPS amplitude to obtain the final amplitude NN(n)×⟨n|Ψ⟩.  In VMC
optimization, the fTNS tensors are initialized using SU, and the NN model parameters are initialized randomly.

   In Markov Chain VMC calculations, a sequence of configurations that differ locally is typically generated, for example, during
the evaluation of local energies for a local Hamiltonian.  In the boundary-MPS contraction of TN amplitudes corresponding
to these configurations, a significant portion of the computation—associated with partial TN contractions—can be reused, as
illustrated in Fig.S3.
   As shown in Fig.S3(a), for a pure fTNS, each local tensor depends only on its on-site configuration. Consequently, modifying
the configurations in the third row does not affect the environment tensor networks⟨E                  2|and|E           4⟩, which can thus be reused for
contracting amplitudes⟨E                           2| ˆO3|E 4⟩that differ solely in the third-row tensors.
   In  contrast,  as  shown  in  Fig.S3(b),  for  a  NN-fPEPS  with  finite  neural  network  rangeR(withR=1  illustrated),  each
local tensor depends not only on its own configuration but also on those of neighboring sites within rangeR.  Consequently,
configuration changes in the third row also affect tensors in the second and fourth rows. The environment tensor networks⟨E                  1|
and|E          5⟩remain unaffected and can be reused for contracting amplitudes⟨E                                         1| ˆO2 ˆO3 ˆO4|E 5⟩, where only the tensors in rows 2 to 4
are varied.

   For  the  pure  fTNS  model  and  NN-fTNS  model  without  TN  contraction  reuse,  the  computational  cost  of  each  VMC
optimization step scales  quadratically asO(N           2) with system  sizeN.   The  contraction of a single-layer  amplitude⟨n|Ψ⟩has
costO(N), which is the dominant cost in the computation of NN-fTNS amplitudes. Each VMC iteration mainly consists of two
parts: sampling new configurations and computing local energies. In the sequential exchange sampling algorithm adopted in this
work [60], one sequential sweep over the system requiresO(N) local configuration updates to generate a new configuration. Each
local update, without TN contraction reuse, has costO(N), leading to an overall sampling cost ofO(N                              2) per sweep.  For local
Hamiltonians such as the Fermi-Hubbard model, computing the local energy for a given configuration|n⟩requires evaluating
amplitudes forO(N) connected configurations|n                                                  c⟩, where⟨n                  c|H|n⟩,0, again leading toO(N                          2) cost when TN contraction reuse
is not employed.  Including the cost of boundary-MPS contraction for a single-layer amplitude TN, which scales asO(D           4χ2)
(withDthe fTNS bond dimension andχthe auxiliary bond dimension), and adopting the typical choiceχ=O(D), the total
leading cost per VMC step becomesO(MD                          6N2) [19], whereMdenotes the Monte Carlo sample size.
   In practice, the VMC sampling can be efficiently parallelized by distributing multiple Markov chains across CPU cores in an
MPI program, reducing the wall-clock time toO(MD          6N2/N   chain), whereN              chain is the number of parallel chains. In this work, we
typically useMof a few tens of thousands andN                     chain≈500.
   For fTNS based models where local tensors only have local configuration dependence, partial TN contraction can be reused
for computation of related amplitudes, as illustrated in Fig.S3.  For pure fTNS, this reuse strategy allows the VMC cost to be
reduced fromO(N                 2) to nearlyO(N) in principle.  For example, in the computation of the local energy for a configuration|n⟩
using boundary MPS contraction, one may first cache all partial TN contraction intermediates (environment MPS|E             i⟩in Fig.S3)
for⟨n|Ψ⟩.  Then, to compute⟨n                                         c|Ψ⟩for a locally connected configuration|n                                       c⟩, only a three-row TN⟨E            i−1 | ˆOi|E i+1 ⟩needs to be
contracted, which scales asO(  √N) for a square lattice.  If additional TN reuse along the column direction is employed, each
|n c⟩requires contracting only a small plaquette TN with constant costC           nc  independent of system size, reducing the total cost
for local energy evaluation toO(C           ncN) [6,44,60].  For NN-fTNS models with finite neural network rangeR, where each local
tensor depends on neighboring configurations within rangeR, a similar partial TN contraction reuse scheme can be applied.  A
comparison of the VMC cost scaling for the above mentioned models as well as conventional fermionic NQS is listed in Table.I.
   As illustrated in Fig.S5for a 6×6 square lattice, TN reuse (applied along the row direction) significantly reduces the VMC
step cost for pure fPEPS across various bond dimensions,  while yielding a more modest cost reduction for NN-fPEPS with
neural network rangeR=1 at larger bond dimensions.  We expect the benefit of TN reuse for NN-fPEPS (R=1) to become
more pronounced as the system size increases.  As discussed in the main text, we emphasize that even employing a minimal
finite-range neural network withR=1 yields ground-state energy accuracies that are substantially better than those achieved by
pure fTNS.
