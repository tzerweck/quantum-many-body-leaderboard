                   Neural-network variational Monte Carlo (NNVMC) has emerged as a powerful tool for solving
                quantum many-body problems, yet systematic pathways for improving its accuracy remain largely
                heuristic. Here, we introduce a physically motivated basis transformation for NNVMC that enhances
                variational expressivity without increasing the complexity of the neural-network ansatz itself.  By
                formulating the many-body wave function in a Gaussian basis, we introduce a single learnable local-
                ity parameter,α, that reshapes the target ground state into a more learnable representation.  This
                approach introduces minimal computational overhead and can be readily combined with existing
                neural-network architectures.  Using the three-dimensional homogeneous electron gas as a bench-
                mark, we show that the optimized basis transformation consistently lowers the variational energy for
                both FermiNet and message-passing neural-network architectures.  Notably, for the latter, it enables
                a more precise determination of the Fermi liquid to Wigner crystal phase transition.  More broadly,
                our results highlight basis transformation as a new route to improving NNVMC in continuous space,
                showing that accuracy can be enhanced not only by refining the ansatz but also by making the target
                ground state easier to represent.

                    D.    Comparison between Evaluation of Expectation Value and Energy Gradient

   Comparing Eq. (S4) with Eq. (S17), we see that the expectation value and the energy gradient are evaluated using
different Monte Carlo strategies.  For a generic observable, we sample the joint distributionq    θ(x,x  ′) and estimate the
double integral over (x,x  ′) in a single Monte Carlo average.  In contrast, for the energy gradient, we first perform
the integral overx ′, sampled from a distribution proportional toI  α(x,x  ′), to construct the generalized local energy
EL(x).  The remaining integral overxis then evaluated using the marginal distributionp       θ(x).  Below we explain the
reason for using these two different strategies.
                                                                                                                                                              5

   For the evaluation of observable expectation values, the one-step estimator is more efficient.  If one instead used
a  two-step  procedure,  both  the  inner  integral  overx ′  and  the  outer  integral  overxwould  have  to  be  estimated
numerically, leading to two sources of statistical error.  Sampling directly from the joint distributionq          θ(x,x  ′) avoids
this  nested Monte  Carlo  structure and  estimates  the full  double integral  in  a single  average,  thereby reducing  the
statistical noise.
   For  the  energy  gradient,  however,  the  two-step  procedure  is  essential  for  obtaining  a  stable  estimator.   By  first
integrating overx ′, we define the generalized local energyE             L(x), which plays the same stabilizing role as the local
energy in conventional VMC. The exact ground state satisfies the generalized eigenvalue equation in the nonorthogonal
basis,

                                              dx ′Hα(x,x  ′)ψ  GS(x′) =E    GS           dx ′Iα(x,x  ′)ψ  GS(x′).

α, the distribution ofx  ′ remains sufficiently localized aroundx, so a modest value ofN      ′ is adequate.
   Because bothNandN     ′  can be kept small in step II, the per-epoch cost remains controlled.  In addition, single-
parameter  optimization  converges  faster  and  more  stably,  so  the  total  number  of  epochs  required  for  convergence
is also limited.  In practice, for FermiNet calculations on the 14-electron system, we use 32 parallel Markov chains
and independently sample 100 values ofx ′ for eachx.  For MPNN calculations on the 36-electron system, we use 16
parallel chains and independently sample 40 values ofx ′ for eachx.  With these settings, the per-epoch computational
time is comparable to that of step I. Since convergence in step II typically requires fewer epochs, about 1000 epochs
in our calculations, the overall runtime is significantly shorter than that of step I.
   Since  only  one  parameter  is  optimized  in  step  II,  the  quantum  geometric  tensorSreduces  to  a  scalar.   In  this
case, SR differs from direct gradient descent only by a multiplicative factor, which effectively provides an adaptive
learning-rate rescaling and stabilizes the optimization without problem-specific tuning.
   In principle, the initial value ofαshould correspond to the limitα                0→∞.  In practice, however, it is sufficient to
start from a large but finiteα          0.  The choice ofα    0 has a substantial effect on the convergence rate, since an excessively
large value requires many training epochs beforeαreaches the relevant regime.  We therefore first probe several values
                                                                                                                                                         12

ofα    0,  separated  by  orders  of  magnitude,  using  a  larger  initial  learning  rateη  ′0.   For  example,  for  FermiNet  with
ndet = 1 atr  s = 5, we find that starting fromα         0 = 103 leads to a monotonic decrease ofαthroughout the probing
run.  Reducing the initial value toα    0 = 102, we observe thatαbegins to oscillate after reaching approximately 2×10                 1.
Based on this observation, we chooseα      0 = 3×10      1 for the final Step-II training and reduce the initial learning rate
fromη   ′0 toη    0.  In our calculations,η       ′0/η0 is typically between 2 and 10.

For  most  calculations,  the  learning  rateηis  kept  constant  in  the  range  0.2–0.4,  and  the  norm  constraintCis

                                              A.    Effectiveness of Two-Step Optimization

   To  illustrate  the  effectiveness  of  the  two-step  optimization  strategy,  we  present  representative  optimization  tra-
jectories for both the energy and the basis parameter.  We consider two cases:  an MPNN ansatz with 36 electrons
atr  s  =  50,  using  two  message-passing  iterations  and  the  plane-wave  reference  state,  and  a  FermiNet  ansatz  with
14 electrons atr  s  = 10 andn       det  = 16.  The results are summarized in Fig.S1.  FiguresS1(a) andS1(b) show the
energy evolution during step I, where only the wave-function parametersθ        1 are optimized.  In both cases, the energy
decreases rapidly at the beginning and then gradually saturates, leaving only residual fluctuations as the optimization
converges.  In step II, we fixθ     1 at the values obtained from step I and optimize only the basis parameterα, initialized
from  a  relatively  large  valueα        0.   As  shown  in  Figs.S1(c)  andS1(d),αfirst  decreases  sharply  and  then  stabilizes
within a finite range, indicating that the single-parameter optimization is stable.
   To  directly  assess  the  effect  ofαon  the  total  energy,  we  examine  the  energyEas  a  function  ofα.   As  noted
earlier, after the basis transformation the energy gradient requires separate Monte Carlo sampling overxandx    ′; this
introduces additional statistical error due to finite sampling ofx ′, making the energy expectation values evaluated at
each epoch less reliable.  Therefore, we cannot directly obtain a smooth curve ofEversus epoch number.  Instead,
because only a single parameterαis involved, we discretely sample a set ofαvalues near the stable region and compute
the corresponding energiesEusing a single Monte Carlo integration (over bothxandx          ′).  Taking the example of
FermiNet with 14 electrons,r        s = 10, andn       det = 16, Fig.S2shows the update ofαand the energyEas a function of
α.  In Fig.S2(a), we initializeαfrom two different values,α                                           0 = 1 andα       0 = 4.  In both cases,αconverges to a range
between 2.2 and 2.6.  Correspondingly, Fig.S2(b) shows the energyEas a function ofα; the range obtained from the
αupdate coincides with the minimum of the energy–αcurve, confirming the effectiveness of theαupdate.


                                                    B.    Failure of Further Optimization

   In the two-step optimization protocol, we first fixα→∞and optimize the wave-function parametersθ                                   1, and then
fixθ     1  and optimize the basis parameterα.  A natural extension is to add a third step,  in whichαis fixed at the
optimized valueα  ∗ obtained from step II andθ       1 is optimized again.  This additional step is, however, computationally
expensive.  Since the wave-function network contains a large number of parameters, stable optimization ofθ    1 requires
a large number ofxsamples, comparable to that used in step I. At the same time, after step II the optimizedα    ∗ is
finite, so an accurate evaluation of the inner integral overx ′  requires a sufficiently large numberN ′  ofx ′  samples.
Because the cost of evaluating the energy gradient scales asO(NN   ′), the per-epoch cost of this third step becomes
prohibitively high.
   Moreover, we find that the energy improvement obtained from step III is not robust.  To illustrate this, we tested
step III using the FermiNet architecture for the unpolarized 14-electron system atr  s = 10, with different values of
ndet.  We used 1024 parallel Markov chains and independently sampled 100 values ofx ′  for eachx,  while keeping
