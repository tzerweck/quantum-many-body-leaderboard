                   Neural  quantum  states  have  emerged  as  a  widely  used  approach  to  the  numerical  study  of  the
                ground states of non-stoquastic Hamiltonians.  However, existing approaches often rely on a  priori
                knowledge  of  the  sign  structure  or  require  a  separately  pre-trained  phase  network.   We  introduce
                a  modiﬁed  stochastic  reconﬁguration  method  that  eﬀectively  u ses  diﬀering  imaginary  time  steps
                to  evolve  the  amplitude  and  phase.  Using  a  larger  time  step  for  phase  optimization,  this  method
                enables a simultaneous and eﬃcient training of phase and ampl itude neural networks.  The eﬃcacy
                of our method is demonstrated on the Heisenberg J1 − J2  model.

1.4     Nesterov’s  accelerated gradient  method
Nesterov’s accelerated gradient (NAG), a seminal advancement in optimization algorithms, has emerged
as a cornerstone in the realm of machine learning and numerical optimization.  NAG can be viewed as
the discrete version of a second-order ordinary diﬀerential equation  (ODE) [4],

                                                  d2θ        dθ
                                                  dt2  + 3t  dt  + ∇f(θ) = 0,                                                         (S19)
where  the  time  parameter t  relates  to  the  step  size.   This  formulation  reveals  an  intuitive  physical
interpretation:  the optimization dynamics resemble a damped system.  At small t, the system is over-
damped, smoothly converging to the equilibrium, while at largert, the system becomes underdamped,
exhibiting oscillations with gradually diminishing amplitude.
    Given an objective function f(θ) to be minimized,  the Nesterov’s accelerated gradient update [5]
is given by

                                                ⃗vt+1 =µ⃗vt −ϵ∇f(⃗θt +µ⃗vt),
                                                ⃗θt+1 =⃗θt +⃗vt+1,                                                                          (S20)

where ϵ> 0 is the learning rate, µ ∈ [0, 1] is the momentum coeﬃcient. ⃗θt  and⃗vt  are the parameter
and  momentum  at  optimization  step t,  respectively.   A  key  diﬀerence  between  NAG  and  classical
momentum  (CM)  lies  in  the  gradient  evaluation:  NAG  computes  ∇f(⃗θt +µ⃗vt),  eﬀectively  ”looking
ahead”  in  the  momentum  direction,  whereas  CM  uses  ∇f(⃗θt)  from  the  current  position θt.   This
subtle modiﬁcation enhances NAG’s responsiveness, leading to more  stable convergence, particularly
for higher values of µ [5].
    Beyond  direct  application,  alternative  approaches  have  been  proposed  to  integrate  momentum
methods  into  stochastic  reconﬁguration  framework  [2,  3].  These  methods  demonstrate  the  ability  of
momentum-based optimization in neural quantum states settings.

1.5     Adaptive  learning  rate

inﬂuenced  by  the  choice  of  the  learning  rate:  small  values  imply  slow  convergence  rate,  while  large

2.1     Background
Consider a space group G with a subgroup T =∑                      n{E|tn} of lattice translations written in the left
coset decomposition,

the symmetries of Bloch wave vectors and hence in classifying space group representations.

                                              {S|w}Ψk(x) = ΨSk({S|w}−1x).                                                   (S31)

consists of Bloch functions characterized by k1, k2,..., kd.  Since these Bloch functions are eigenstates
of T, in this basis the elements of T are represented by diagonal matrices with diagonal elements given
by e−ikpt  (p = 1 to d).

isogonal  point  group  F.  For  some  vector  k1  ∈  1BZ,  we  deﬁne  the  star  of  k1  to  be  a  set  of  distinct

In order to study physical models in reality,  we construct supercells with lattice vectors that consist
